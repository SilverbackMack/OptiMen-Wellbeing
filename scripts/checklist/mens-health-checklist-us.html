The Men's Health Checklist

The checks worth having at every age, what's usually covered, and how to get each one. Tick them off as you go.

ANY AGE Start here

  • Book a yearly preventive visit.USUALLY NO COPAY Most private plans and Medicare cover recommended screenings at no cost when you see an in-network doctor. Check your plan.
  • Know your blood pressure. High blood pressure usually has no symptoms. US guidelines count 130/80 or more as high. Many pharmacies have a free machine.
  • Know what's normal for your testicles. Doctors don't screen for testicular cancer, but see one about any lump, swelling, change in shape or ache.
  • Ask about your family history. Find out whether close relatives have had heart disease, stroke, diabetes, or prostate, breast, ovarian or colon cancer, and at what age.
  • Be honest about how you're coping. If you've had "nothing left to give" for weeks, talk to your doctor. Your job may offer free counseling through an Employee Assistance Program.
  • Get tested once for hepatitis C and HIV.ADULTS Recommended at least once for most adults, usually with a simple blood test.

FROM 35 Heart and metabolism

  • Cholesterol test. Every 4 to 6 years for most adults, more often if you have risk factors. Ask your doctor for your 10-year heart risk.
  • Blood sugar test, if you're overweight.AGES 35 TO 70 Checks for prediabetes and type 2 diabetes, which often have no symptoms.
  • Sleep check-in. See a doctor if poor sleep has lasted for months, or if you snore loudly and someone has noticed pauses in your breathing.

FROM 40 TO 55 Prostate

  • From 40 to 45, if you're at higher risk: talk to your doctor about a PSA test. Higher risk means you're Black, or your father or brother had prostate cancer before 65.
  • From 50 to 55: decide with your doctor whether to test. The American Cancer Society suggests talking about it from 50. The US Preventive Services Task Force says it's a personal choice from 55 to 69.
  • Getting up at night to pee, or a weak flow? It's usually a non-cancerous enlarged prostate, but only a check-up can tell.

FROM 45 Screening

  • Colorectal cancer screening.AGES 45 TO 75 A home stool test every 1 to 3 years, or a colonoscopy every 10 years. Ask which suits you.
  • Lung cancer scan, if you've smoked heavily.AGES 50 TO 80 A yearly low-dose CT scan if you have a 20 pack-year history and smoke now or quit in the last 15 years.
  • AAA scan, if you've ever smoked.AGES 65 TO 75 A one-time ultrasound of the main artery in your belly.
Based on US Preventive Services Task Force, CDC, American Heart Association and American Cancer Society guidance. Coverage depends on your plan.

Before any appointment

  • Write down your main concern in one sentence. Say it first. Appointments are short.
  • List your medicines and supplements. Include creatine, which changes a kidney blood test, and finasteride, which lowers PSA.
  • Before a PSA blood test: no ejaculation or vigorous exercise, including cycling, for 48 hours.
  • Ask three questions: What are my numbers? What do they mean for me? What happens next, and when?

Five habits that move the numbers

  • Move most days. Aim for 150 minutes a week of activity that raises your breathing, plus strength work on 2 days.
  • Keep a regular bedtime. Most adults need at least 7 hours.
  • Drink less. If you drink, 2 drinks or fewer a day, and less is better.
  • Don't smoke. Free help to quit: call 1-800-QUIT-NOW (1-800-784-8669).
  • Eat fish twice a week, and fill half your plate with vegetables and fruit.

Don't wait for a check-up

  • Call 911 for sudden chest pain or pressure that doesn't go away, or signs of a stroke: a drooping face, arm weakness or slurred speech.
  • Go to the ER or call 911 for sudden, severe pain in a testicle.
  • See a doctor promptly about blood in your pee, poop or semen, a lump anywhere, or weight loss you can't explain.
  • Struggling right now? Call or text 988, the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, any time.
This checklist is general health information for men in the US, based on US Preventive Services Task Force, CDC, American Heart Association and American Cancer Society guidance as of November 2026. It is not medical advice and doesn't replace a conversation with your doctor. Guidance changes, so check with your doctor for the latest.
